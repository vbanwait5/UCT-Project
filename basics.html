1. Qubits and amplitudes

We begin with one qubit, the quantum analogue of a bit. A classical bit is either 0 or 1. A qubit may instead be in a weighted combination of the two basis states |0⟩ and |1⟩.

Definition 1 (Qubit)

A one-qubit state has the form

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩,   where   |α|² + |β|² = 1.

The complex numbers α and β are called amplitudes.

This notation is called ket notation. Nothing mysterious is hidden by it: after identifying kets with column vectors, we have

|0⟩ = [1, 0]ᵀ,    |1⟩ = [0, 1]ᵀ,    |ψ⟩ = [α, β]ᵀ.

The normalization condition will ensure that the probabilities of all measurement outcomes sum to one. Notice, however, that amplitudes are not themselves probabilities. They may be negative or genuinely complex, and their relative phases can influence later interference even when their magnitudes agree.

Multiplying every amplitude by the same complex phase does not change any measurement probability; this is called an irrelevant global phase. A phase difference between terms, however, can change how they interfere.

One useful caution (Superposition depends on the basis)

The state |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 is a superposition in the computational basis {|0⟩, |1⟩}. In the basis {|+⟩, |−⟩}, it is simply one basis state. We will normally measure in the computational basis unless stated otherwise.

2. Measurement

A state vector is not a classical record that we may inspect coordinate by coordinate. To obtain a classical bit, we measure the qubit in the computational basis.

Rule 2 (The Born rule)

If |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, measuring returns 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|². After outcome b is observed, the state is |b⟩.

Thus measurement converts amplitudes into probabilities by taking squared magnitudes:

  • outcome 0 occurs with probability |α|²;
  • outcome 1 occurs with probability |β|².

Conditioned on observing b, the state becomes |b⟩. An immediate repetition of the same measurement therefore returns b with probability one.

Example (Unequal amplitudes)

For |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩, the outcome probabilities are 3/4 and 1/4. Squaring the amplitudes—not reading them directly—produces the probabilities.

Check your understanding: can one measurement recover α and β?

No. A single measurement returns one classical outcome. Repeated computational-basis measurements on independently prepared copies estimate |α|² and |β|², but cannot distinguish states such as |+⟩ and |−⟩. Recovering an unknown state requires measurements in additional bases; this task is quantum state tomography.

3. Phase and interference

The measurement rule might suggest that only |α| and |β| matter. That conclusion is false: although a common global phase has no observable effect, a relative phase can change the outcome of a later computation. Consider

|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2   and   |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2.

Computational-basis measurement gives 0 and 1 with equal probability for either state. Nevertheless, |+⟩ and |−⟩ are orthogonal: ⟨+|−⟩ = 0. They cannot differ by a global phase. Applying a Hadamard gate maps them to |0⟩ and |1⟩, making the relative sign perfectly distinguishable.

Idea (Interference)

If a unitary maps several basis components into the same output basis component, linearity makes their complex amplitudes add before squared magnitudes are taken. Equal-phase contributions increase the resulting magnitude; opposite contributions cancel. “Interference” is this ordinary addition of amplitudes viewed computationally.

We can now see why a coherent quantum state contains more operational structure than a classical random choice with the same immediate measurement probabilities. Classical probabilities only add; quantum amplitudes may add or cancel. Algorithm design consists in arranging those additions so that unwanted alternatives cancel and the desired invariant survives.

4. Multiple qubits

Simon’s algorithm uses registers rather than isolated qubits. Two qubits have four computational-basis states—|00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, and |11⟩—so their joint state is a weighted combination of those four possibilities:

|ψ⟩ = α00|00⟩ + α01|01⟩ + α10|10⟩ + α11|11⟩,
with   |α00|² + |α01|² + |α10|² + |α11|² = 1.

More generally, the basis states of an n-qubit register are indexed by the 2n strings x ∈ {0,1}n. Every state can therefore be written as

|ψ⟩ = ∑x∈{0,1}ⁿ αx|x⟩,    ∑x|αx|² = 1.

Measuring all qubits in the computational basis returns x with probability |αx|² and leaves state |x⟩. Although a generic state requires 2n amplitudes to describe classically, measurement does not expose that description.

When qubits cannot be described separately

If qubit A is in state |ψ⟩ and qubit B is in state |φ⟩ independently, their joint state is |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩, abbreviated |ψ⟩|φ⟩. Bilinearity determines the product; for example,

(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) ⊗ (γ|0⟩ + δ|1⟩) = αγ|00⟩ + αδ|01⟩ + βγ|10⟩ + βδ|11⟩.

Definition 3 (Entanglement)

A joint state is entangled when it cannot be written as one state for the first system times one state for the second. For example, (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 is entangled: the two qubits have a joint description, but neither qubit has its own independent pure state.

5. Quantum gates

We now know how to describe a register and how to read it. The remaining ingredient is evolution. A closed quantum system evolves linearly through a unitary operator. A square complex matrix U is unitary when U†U = I; equivalently, it preserves inner products. It therefore preserves norms and is reversible, with U−1 = U†.

Definition 4 (Quantum gate)

A quantum gate on k qubits is a unitary operator U on (ℂ2)⊗k, represented in the computational basis by a 2k × 2k unitary matrix. When applied to selected qubits of a larger register, it acts as U on those factors and as the identity on all others.

The Pauli-X gate

X = 0110,    X|0⟩ = |1⟩,    X|1⟩ = |0⟩.

The X gate acts like a classical NOT on computational-basis states. On a superposition, it swaps the amplitudes of |0⟩ and |1⟩.

The Hadamard gate

H = 1/√2111−1,    H|0⟩ = |+⟩,    H|1⟩ = |−⟩.

The Hadamard gate changes between the computational basis and the {|+⟩, |−⟩} basis. From a basis state it creates an equal-magnitude superposition, but the same gate can also undo that superposition: H|+⟩ = |0⟩ and H|−⟩ = |1⟩. The identity H² = I is our first concrete example of interference reversing a preparation step.

Key identity (Hadamard transform on n qubits)

For every x ∈ {0,1}n,

H⊗n|x⟩ = 1/√(2n) ∑z∈{0,1}ⁿ (−1)x·z|z⟩,

where x · z = ∑i=1n xizi is computed modulo 2.

Why is the Hadamard identity true?

For one bit b, H|b⟩ = (|0⟩ + (−1)b|1⟩)/√2. Applying this rule independently to all n positions produces one term |z⟩ for every bit string z. Multiplying the signs contributed by its 1-bits gives (−1)x·z. This is the only form of the Hadamard transform used later.

The special case x = 0n produces the uniform superposition 2−n/2∑z|z⟩. The phase factor (−1)x·z is the algebraic source of the interference used by Simon’s algorithm.

The controlled-NOT gate

CNOT acts on two qubits. On computational-basis states it flips the target bit exactly when the control bit is 1, and linearity specifies its action on every superposition:

CNOT |a,b⟩ = |a, b ⊕ a⟩.

For example, applying CNOT to |+⟩|0⟩ = (|00⟩ + |10⟩)/√2 produces the entangled Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2.

Check your understanding: what happens if H is applied twice to |0⟩?

The first H gives (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2. On the second application, the |1⟩ contributions cancel while the |0⟩ contributions reinforce, returning |0⟩. You can verify this in the state laboratory above.

6. The toolkit we will carry forward

The rest of the site uses the following five facts. If these make sense, you have enough quantum background to continue:

  1. An n-qubit state assigns one complex amplitude to each n-bit string, and the squared magnitudes sum to one.
  2. Measurement returns one bit string according to those squared magnitudes.
  3. Quantum gates are reversible linear operations that preserve total probability.
  4. Relative phase affects the addition of amplitudes and can be converted into observable probabilities.
  5. Hadamard gates create the signs that Simon’s interference step will add or cancel.

In Simon’s algorithm these ingredients appear in exactly this order. Hadamard gates prepare a uniform superposition, an oracle query correlates inputs according to the hidden shift, and a second Hadamard layer converts relative phase into measurable linear constraints. The next chapter packages these operations into the circuit and oracle model.

Takeaway

The circuit model alternates unitary evolution with measurement. Its state space is a tensor product; its dynamics are linear; its output probabilities follow the Born rule. Simon’s algorithm is an explicit construction inside this model, not an appeal to quantum parallelism alone.
