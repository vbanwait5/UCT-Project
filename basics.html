1. Qubits and amplitudes

We work over the field ℂ. For a vector |ψ⟩, its conjugate transpose is denoted ⟨ψ|, and its Euclidean norm is ‖|ψ⟩‖ = √⟨ψ|ψ⟩. The state space of one qubit is the two-dimensional complex Hilbert space ℂ2 with distinguished computational basis {|0⟩, |1⟩}.

Definition 1 (Qubit)

A pure state of one qubit is represented by a unit vector |ψ⟩ ∈ ℂ2. Relative to the computational basis, it has the unique expansion

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩,   where   |α|² + |β|² = 1.

The complex numbers α and β are called amplitudes.

The notation |ψ⟩ is called ket notation. Identifying kets with column vectors gives

|0⟩ = [1, 0]ᵀ,    |1⟩ = [0, 1]ᵀ,    |ψ⟩ = [α, β]ᵀ.

Normalization ensures that measurement probabilities sum to one. The amplitudes themselves are not probabilities: they may be negative or complex, and their relative phase affects later computation.

Definition 2 (Physical equivalence and global phase)

Unit vectors |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩ represent the same pure state exactly when |φ⟩ = eiθ|ψ⟩ for some θ ∈ ℝ. The factor eiθ is a global phase. Thus a physical pure state is formally a ray, rather than a particular unit vector.

Remark 3 (Superposition is basis-dependent)

The state |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 is a superposition in the computational basis {|0⟩, |1⟩}. In the basis {|+⟩, |−⟩}, it is simply one basis state. We will normally measure in the computational basis unless stated otherwise.

Scope (Pure and mixed states)

The general state of a quantum system is a density operator ρ: a positive semidefinite operator with tr(ρ) = 1. A state is pure exactly when ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ| for some unit vector |ψ⟩. Because an ideal circuit initialized in a basis state remains pure until measurement, state vectors are sufficient for our treatment; density operators become necessary for classical mixtures, noise, or subsystems of entangled states.

2. Measurement

A quantum state is not directly readable. The standard computational-basis measurement of one qubit is the projective measurement {Π0, Π1}, where Πb = |b⟩⟨b| for b ∈ {0,1}.

Definition 4 (Projective measurement)

A projective measurement is a finite collection of orthogonal projectors {Πj} satisfying Πj† = Πj, ΠjΠk = 0 for j ≠ k, and ∑jΠj = I. On unit state |ψ⟩, outcome j occurs with probability ‖Πj|ψ⟩‖². Conditioned on that outcome, the post-measurement state is Πj|ψ⟩ / ‖Πj|ψ⟩‖, provided the denominator is nonzero.

Applying this rule to |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ gives a classical bit:

  • outcome 0 occurs with probability |α|²;
  • outcome 1 occurs with probability |β|².

Conditioned on observing b, the state becomes |b⟩. An immediate repetition of the same measurement therefore returns b with probability one.

Example 5 (Unequal amplitudes)

For |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩, the outcome probabilities are 3/4 and 1/4. Squaring the amplitudes—not reading them directly—produces the probabilities.

Check your understanding: can one measurement recover α and β?

No. A single measurement returns one classical outcome. Repeated computational-basis measurements on independently prepared copies estimate |α|² and |β|², but cannot distinguish states such as |+⟩ and |−⟩. Recovering an unknown state requires measurements in additional bases; this task is quantum state tomography.

3. Phase and interference

Global phase has no observable effect, but relative phase does. Consider the two states

|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2   and   |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2.

Computational-basis measurement gives 0 and 1 with equal probability for either state. Nevertheless, |+⟩ and |−⟩ are orthogonal: ⟨+|−⟩ = 0. They cannot differ by a global phase. Applying a Hadamard gate maps them to |0⟩ and |1⟩, making the relative sign perfectly distinguishable.

Idea (Interference)

If a unitary maps several basis components into the same output basis component, linearity makes their complex amplitudes add before squared magnitudes are taken. Equal-phase contributions increase the resulting magnitude; opposite contributions cancel. “Interference” is this ordinary addition of amplitudes viewed computationally.

This is why a quantum state is richer than a classical random choice. Probabilities never cancel, but amplitudes can. Quantum algorithms are designed so wrong or irrelevant answers cancel while useful structure is reinforced.

4. Multiple qubits

If systems A and B have state spaces ℋA and ℋB, their joint state space is the tensor product ℋA ⊗ ℋB. Consequently, two qubits live in ℂ2 ⊗ ℂ2 ≅ ℂ4, with computational basis |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩.

|ψ⟩ = α00|00⟩ + α01|01⟩ + α10|10⟩ + α11|11⟩,
with   |α00|² + |α01|² + |α10|² + |α11|² = 1.

More generally, the state space of n qubits is (ℂ2)⊗n, a 2n-dimensional complex vector space. Its computational basis is indexed by strings x ∈ {0,1}n, so every pure state has the form

|ψ⟩ = ∑x∈{0,1}ⁿ αx|x⟩,    ∑x|αx|² = 1.

Measuring all qubits in the computational basis returns x with probability |αx|² and leaves state |x⟩. Although a generic state requires 2n amplitudes to describe classically, measurement does not expose that description.

Product states and entanglement

If qubit A is in state |ψ⟩ and qubit B is in state |φ⟩ independently, their joint state is |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩, abbreviated |ψ⟩|φ⟩. Bilinearity determines the product; for example,

(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) ⊗ (γ|0⟩ + δ|1⟩) = αγ|00⟩ + αδ|01⟩ + βγ|10⟩ + βδ|11⟩.

Definition 6 (Separable and entangled pure states)

A bipartite pure state |ψ⟩ ∈ ℋA ⊗ ℋB is separable if |ψ⟩ = |a⟩ ⊗ |b⟩ for unit vectors |a⟩ ∈ ℋA and |b⟩ ∈ ℋB. Otherwise it is entangled. The Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 is entangled.

To see why the Bell state is entangled, suppose its amplitudes factored as (α|0⟩ + β|1⟩)(γ|0⟩ + δ|1⟩). The nonzero |00⟩ and |11⟩ coefficients require αγ ≠ 0 and βδ ≠ 0, while the zero |01⟩ and |10⟩ coefficients require αδ = βγ = 0—a contradiction.

5. Quantum gates

A closed quantum system evolves linearly by a unitary operator. A square complex matrix U is unitary when U†U = I. Equivalently, it preserves inner products: ⟨Uψ|Uφ⟩ = ⟨ψ|φ⟩. In particular, it preserves norms and is reversible, with U−1 = U†.

Definition 7 (Quantum gate)

A quantum gate on k qubits is a unitary operator U on (ℂ2)⊗k, represented in the computational basis by a 2k × 2k unitary matrix. When applied to selected qubits of a larger register, it acts as U on those factors and as the identity on all others.

The Pauli-X gate

X = 0110,    X|0⟩ = |1⟩,    X|1⟩ = |0⟩.

The X gate acts like a classical NOT on computational-basis states. On a superposition, it swaps the amplitudes of |0⟩ and |1⟩.

The Hadamard gate

H = 1/√2111−1,    H|0⟩ = |+⟩,    H|1⟩ = |−⟩.

The Hadamard gate moves between the computational basis and the {|+⟩, |−⟩} basis. It creates an equal superposition from a basis state, but it can also remove one: H|+⟩ = |0⟩ and H|−⟩ = |1⟩. Thus H² = I.

Proposition 8 (Hadamard transform on n qubits)

For every x ∈ {0,1}n,

H⊗n|x⟩ = 1/√(2n) ∑z∈{0,1}ⁿ (−1)x·z|z⟩,

where x · z = ∑i=1n xizi is computed modulo 2.

Proof. For a single bit b, H|b⟩ = (|0⟩ + (−1)b|1⟩)/√2. Taking the tensor product over positions i ∈ {1,…,n} and expanding by multilinearity gives one term |z⟩ for each z ∈ {0,1}n. Its coefficient is 2−n/2∏i(−1)xizi = 2−n/2(−1)x·z. ∎

The special case x = 0n produces the uniform superposition 2−n/2∑z|z⟩. The phase factor (−1)x·z is the algebraic source of the interference used by Simon’s algorithm.

The controlled-NOT gate

CNOT acts on two qubits. On computational-basis states it flips the target bit exactly when the control bit is 1, and linearity specifies its action on every superposition:

CNOT |a,b⟩ = |a, b ⊕ a⟩.

For example, applying CNOT to |+⟩|0⟩ = (|00⟩ + |10⟩)/√2 produces the entangled Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2.

Check your understanding: what happens if H is applied twice to |0⟩?

The first H gives (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2. On the second application, the |1⟩ contributions cancel while the |0⟩ contributions reinforce, returning |0⟩. You can verify this in the state laboratory above.

6. The toolkit we will carry forward

The essential ideas can now be summarized compactly:

  1. An n-qubit pure state is a unit vector in (ℂ2)⊗n, modulo global phase.
  2. A projective measurement obeys the Born rule and changes the post-measurement state.
  3. Closed-system evolution is unitary, hence linear, norm-preserving, and reversible.
  4. Relative phase affects the addition of amplitudes and can be converted into observable probabilities.
  5. A joint pure state is entangled exactly when it does not factor across the relevant tensor-product partition.

In Simon’s algorithm, Hadamard gates will create a uniform superposition, an oracle query will encode the hidden shift, and another layer of Hadamards will use interference to reveal linear constraints on that shift. Every step rests on the definitions above.

Takeaway

The circuit model alternates unitary evolution with measurement. Its state space is a tensor product; its dynamics are linear; its output probabilities follow the Born rule. Simon’s algorithm is an explicit construction inside this model, not an appeal to quantum parallelism alone.
