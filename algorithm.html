Chapter 05

Simon’s Algorithm

The algorithm never tries to read all oracle values. It prepares a coherent superposition of one hidden pair and uses Hadamard interference to turn that pair’s translation symmetry into a linear constraint.

25–30 minute read

1. The algorithm at one level of abstraction

Assume oracle access to a Simon function with hidden nonzero s ∈ 𝔽2n. The goal of one sampling round is modest: rather than recover s immediately, it will produce one random equation satisfied by s. The round proceeds as follows.

  1. Initialize two n-qubit registers to |0n⟩|0n⟩.
  2. Apply H⊗n to the first register.
  3. Apply the oracle Uf.
  4. Ignore or measure the second register.
  5. Apply H⊗n to the first register and measure it, obtaining z.

The essential invariant is that every returned vector z satisfies z · s = 0 over 𝔽2. One equation cannot identify s, but independent repetitions build a homogeneous linear system. Once its row space has dimension n−1, its nullspace is one-dimensional and must equal span{s}.

Algorithm 1 (Simon sampling)

repeat until the sampled equations have rank n−1: run the quantum sampling round and append the measured row z. Then compute the unique nonzero vector in the nullspace over 𝔽2.

2. Prepare and query

Let us now derive that invariant rather than assume it. The two registers begin in |0n⟩|0n⟩. Applying a Hadamard to every qubit of the first register prepares the uniform superposition

|ψ1⟩ = 1/√(2n) ∑x∈𝔽₂ⁿ |x⟩|0n⟩.

The oracle then acts once:

|ψ2⟩ = 1/√(2n) ∑x∈𝔽₂ⁿ |x⟩|f(x)⟩.

The query correlates each input with its oracle value. By the Simon promise, the terms indexed by x and x ⊕ s carry the same label in the second register, and no third term carries that label. Thus the joint state is naturally grouped into coherent pairs.

Important (Superposition is not the conclusion)

At this point, measuring both registers would reveal one ordinary pair (x, f(x)) and waste the structure. The useful step is to erase which-pair information while preserving coherence between the two members of a fiber.

3. Isolating a coset state

To make one such pair explicit, suppose we measure the second register and observe f(x0). Exactly two terms are compatible with this outcome, so after normalization the first register becomes

|Cx₀⟩ = (|x0⟩ + |x0 ⊕ s⟩)/√2.

This state is the uniform superposition over the coset x0 + H. The representative x0 is random and carries no stable information across runs. What is stable is the displacement between the two basis states: every possible coset contains two points separated by the same hidden shift s.

Deep insight (The output measurement is optional)

We may discard the second register without measuring it. The first register is then a classical mixture of coset states, and every coset yields the same final distribution on z. Measuring the output is a proof device, not a required source of computational power.

4. Interference exposes orthogonality

The question is now whether a basis change can reveal the common displacement without revealing the random representative. Apply H⊗n to the coset state. Substituting the identity H⊗n|x⟩ = 2−n/2∑z(−1)x·z|z⟩ gives

1/√(2n+1) ∑z (−1)x₀·z[1 + (−1)s·z]|z⟩.

The bracket contains the entire mechanism. If s · z = 1, it becomes 1−1=0 and the two contributions cancel. If s · z = 0, it becomes 1+1=2 and they reinforce. Therefore the measurement can return only vectors in the orthogonal subspace

s⊥ = {z ∈ 𝔽2n : z · s = 0}

can be measured. The phase (−1)x0·z depends on the random coset representative, but its squared magnitude is one, so it does not affect the measurement distribution.

5. Repeat and solve over 𝔽₂

One round therefore produces a uniformly random row z from the (n−1)-dimensional subspace s⊥. Repeating the round converts the quantum problem into an ordinary linear-algebra problem: stack the observed rows into a matrix Z and seek a nonzero solution to

Zs = 0   over 𝔽2.

Once rank(Z) = n−1, its nullspace has dimension one. Since the true s lies in the nullspace, the only nonzero solution is s. Gaussian elimination uses XOR in place of subtraction.

Connection: randomized computation

The quantum circuit is randomized only at measurement. Correctness is one-sided at the equation level: a sampled row never violates z · s = 0. Failure occurs because the rows may be linearly dependent, and repetition reduces that failure probability exponentially.
