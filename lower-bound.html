Chapter 07

Why Classical Algorithms
Need Exponentially Many Queries

A classical query reveals one opaque label. Until two labels match, the hidden shift remains concealed; the birthday paradox says that seeing such a match requires about 2n/2 queries.

20–25 minute read

1. What must be proved?

The O(n)-query quantum upper bound proves that one algorithm is efficient; it does not yet prove a separation. For that, we need a lower bound against every classical randomized algorithm, including adaptive algorithms whose next query may depend on the entire transcript seen so far.

Theorem 1 (Classical randomized query complexity)

Every randomized classical algorithm that solves Simon’s search problem with success probability at least 2/3 on every promised oracle makes Ω(2n/2) queries in the worst case. Moreover, O(2n/2) queries suffice. Hence

R1/3(Simonn) = Θ(2n/2).

The proof formalizes one simple idea: before a collision, the answers look like unrelated labels; after a collision, the secret is immediately known.

2. What information reveals the secret?

Let N=2n and M=N−1. To prevent an algorithm from learning anything from the spelling of an output label, imagine choosing a promised oracle at random:

  1. Choose s uniformly from the M nonzero strings.
  2. The pairs {x,x⊕s} are the N/2 cosets of {0,s}. Assign distinct output labels to these pairs by a uniformly random injection into {0,1}n.

Fix any deterministic algorithm making q distinct queries. As long as all observed outputs are different, every answer is just a fresh random label whose bit pattern is independent of s. A repeated label is different: if f(x)=f(y), then the promise gives s=x⊕y.

Core information fact

Without a collision, many hidden shifts remain consistent with everything the algorithm has seen. A collision reveals the shift exactly.

3. Why a collision needs about √N queries

For a set Q={x1,…,xq} of queried inputs, define its nonzero difference set

D(Q) = {xi⊕xj : 1≤i<j≤q}.

There are at most C=q(q−1)/2 such pairwise differences. The definition is useful because a collision appears exactly when the hidden shift belongs to D(Q): two queried points collide precisely when their XOR equals s.

Collision bound

For every deterministic adaptive q-query algorithm under this random choice of oracle,

Pr[collision] ≤ q(q−1)/[2(N−1)].

Why the bound holds. Imagine following the algorithm while supplying a new label after every query. Because the algorithm is deterministic, those labels determine its entire collision-free query set Q. A real collision can occur only when the random secret s equals one of the at most q(q−1)/2 differences in D(Q). Since s is uniform among N−1 nonzero strings, the displayed probability follows. Notice that the argument still allows each new query to depend on every earlier answer.

If q is much smaller than √N, then q²/N is small, so a collision is unlikely. Without a collision, the algorithm has eliminated at most q(q−1)/2 of the N−1 possible secrets and can do little better than guess. Constant success therefore requires

q = Ω(√N) = Ω(2n/2).

4. Why this also covers randomized algorithms

A randomized algorithm is a probability distribution over deterministic algorithms: fixing its random coins fixes every query it will make. The hard distribution above defeats every such deterministic strategy that uses o(2n/2) queries.

The formal Yao-principle step

Suppose a randomized q-query algorithm succeeded with probability at least 2/3 on every promised oracle. Its average success on the hard distribution μ would also be at least 2/3. Averaging additionally over its random coins means that some fixed choice of coins—hence some deterministic q-query algorithm—would have average success at least 2/3 on μ. The preceding collision argument rules this out when q=o(2n/2). This is Yao’s minimax principle in the form needed here.

Therefore every bounded-error classical randomized algorithm requires Ω(2n/2) queries in the worst case.

5. The matching birthday upper bound

The lower bound is meaningful only if the birthday scale is also achievable. Consider the following classical algorithm: sample q distinct inputs uniformly, store their output labels, and stop when two labels agree. The corresponding inputs then reveal s immediately through their XOR.

There are roughly q²/2 pairs among q queried inputs. Each pair has one chance to differ by the secret s, which is one of about N possibilities. The expected number of collisions therefore becomes constant when q²/N is constant—that is, when q is on the order of √N.

A formal constant-success calculation

For a fixed secret, the domain is a perfect matching of N points. If i collision-free inputs have been chosen, then i of the N−i remaining points are their partners. Hence

Pr[no collision] = ∏i=0q−1(N−2i)/(N−i) ≤ exp(−q(q−1)/(2N)).

Choosing q≥1+√(2N ln 3) makes this probability at most 1/3, so a collision is found with probability at least 2/3.

Consequently R1/3(Simonn)=O(√N)=O(2n/2), matching the lower bound up to constants.

Conclusion

The classical proof is tight: Θ(2n/2) queries are both necessary and sufficient. Together with the O(n)-query quantum algorithm, this establishes the exponential separation.
